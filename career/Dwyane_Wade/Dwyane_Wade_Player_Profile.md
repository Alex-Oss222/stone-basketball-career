## Overview

Dwyane Wade is a shooting guard who wants the defender moving before he attacks. He catches on the wing, gets past the closeout and makes the help choose between his finish and a pass to a teammate. He can start the same sequence after a rebound. His strength lets him keep going when a guard gets alongside him, and he is comfortable finishing with either hand.

He is reserved around people he does not know and more talkative with teammates. In film sessions, he asks about the screener's angle, the help defender and the pass that was available. He takes a precise correction back onto the court. After a bad possession, he can also hurry the next one and give the coach another mistake to discuss.

As of June 25, 2003, Wade has completed three seasons at UConn and is preparing for the NBA draft. He is 19, listed at 6 feet 6 inches and 220 pounds, and has become a much better perimeter shooter. Teams considering him near the top of the draft still have to decide how much point-guard work to give him.

## 1. Background and Education

Dwyane Tyrone Wade Jr. was born January 17, 1984. He will be 19 years, 5 months and 9 days old on June 26, 2003. He comes from the Chicago area, with Robbins as his home community, and attends Harold L. Richards High School in Oak Lawn before enrolling at the University of Connecticut in Storrs.

Two grade accelerations before high school put him at Richards in autumn 1996 at age 12. He completes the required coursework, graduates in spring 2000 at 16 and enters UConn that fall. His academic record allows him to play immediately. By the draft, he has finished his junior year and still intends to complete his degree.

This is an alternate-history player record. The 1984 birthday, accelerated education, UConn career, measurements, statistics, personal incidents and workout results are established fiction for this simulation. Historical sources supply the basketball background and the 2003 draft setting. They do not verify the invented events or describe the real Wade's college career.

## 2. High School

Wade begins as a rotation guard at Richards. In 1996-97, he appears in 24 varsity games and starts 12, averaging 10.8 points. Older defenders can move him off his driving line. He gets easier attempts by running ahead, cutting behind a defender and attacking before the lane fills.

More ball handling follows over the next two seasons. He uses a crossover to change direction and a hesitation to get the defender upright. When the first gap closes, he begins stopping on two feet and passing out. He still picks up the ball too early against pressure. His scoring rises from 17.1 points as a sophomore to 22.9 as a junior, while his turnovers rise with the extra possessions.

His senior season gives college coaches a fuller offensive player. He averages 27.4 points, 7.7 rebounds and 5.9 assists in 30 games, shoots 39.0 percent from three and gets to the line almost seven times a game. Richards finishes 25-5 and loses in a sectional final. Wade leaves school around 205 pounds.

UConn recruits him to play on the wing, defend and earn more responsibility with the ball. The available role appeals to him. His summer work centers on keeping the dribble under pressure and getting his shot away before a longer defender closes the space.

## 3. College at UConn

### Freshman Season, 2000-01

At 16, Wade enters an offense with older players ahead of him. He scores from cuts, transition and catches after the defense has already moved. He plays all 34 games, starts 24 and averages 13.2 points, 4.6 rebounds and 3.6 assists. UConn finishes 28-6 and reaches the Sweet 16.

He can beat the first defender and make the nearby pass. The next decision takes longer. Against a second defender, he sometimes stops his dribble and waits for a teammate to rescue the possession. The staff uses him alongside another handler and lets him attack after the initial action.

### Sophomore Season, 2001-02

Wade starts all 30 games and handles more ball screens. Taliek Brown organizes the offense, Ben Gordon supplies shooting, and Wade takes possessions that require someone to get into the lane. Those responsibilities overlap. In smaller lineups, he also defends college wings.

He averages 16.7 points and 4.8 assists. His free-throw percentage improves to 89.9, while his three-point percentage slips to 37.7 as he takes more difficult attempts. UConn goes 23-7 and again exits in the Sweet 16. Wade is becoming more useful with the ball, though the turnovers that do occur still tend to come when a second defender changes the first read.

### Junior Season, 2002-03

Wade becomes UConn's leading scorer. Brown still brings order to the half-court offense, Gordon draws attention on the perimeter, and Emeka Okafor gives Wade a target rolling to the basket. Wade averages 24.3 points, 7.1 rebounds and 7.2 assists. He makes 76 of 184 threes and 199 of 210 free throws. He earns first-team All-Big East and second-team national All-America recognition in this simulation.

UConn finishes 25-9 and reaches the Elite Eight. Michigan State ends the season with a 79-72 win. Wade scores 22 points on 8-for-20 shooting, with seven rebounds, four assists and six turnovers. Several of those turnovers come after he leaves the floor and finds the intended pass covered. He finishes his college career with seven NCAA Tournament wins in ten games.

## 4. Scoring

### Finishing

Wade is most dangerous catching on the wing against a defender closing toward him. He can rip the ball through, take the first gap and keep the defender on his hip. A ball screen gives him another way to reach that position. Once he gets a shoulder past the guard, his strength makes it difficult to recover without bringing help.

He finishes off one foot when he has a clear path and uses a two-foot gather when he expects contact. He extends with either hand, reverses under the rim and changes the direction of his final steps when the help arrives early. His jumper and free throw are right-handed. His use of the left hand is strongest on finishes and short passes.

The trouble begins when he commits to the gather before reading the help. A defender who establishes position early can make him stop, turn or attempt a difficult finish. Wade sometimes takes the contact when the pass to the corner is already open. He can score on that possession and still have missed the better play.

### Perimeter Shooting

His most dependable three comes from a catch with his feet set. After giving the ball up, he will move a few steps along the perimeter to open a return pass. The defender has to close under control. A hard closeout gives him the drive or a one-dribble pull-up.

The pull-up is most useful from about 12 to 18 feet, particularly when a big defender retreats toward the rim. Wade gets enough elevation to shoot over the recovering guard. His balance becomes less reliable when he stops from full speed or turns sharply into the attempt. Coming off a pin-down, he is more comfortable curling into a two-pointer than turning straight into a three.

He can make a pull-up three or a stepback. Those remain occasional attempts. His junior shooting numbers come from a college offense in which he gets prepared catches as well as shots he creates himself; the percentages do not establish equal accuracy on every type of jumper.

### Post Play

A smaller guard gives him a reason to catch in the mid-post. He takes one or two dribbles, feels the defender's position and turns over either shoulder to shoot right-handed. He will also pass to a cutter when the weak-side defender watches the ball.

He stays too long on some post-ups. Once the second defender has arrived, another backing dribble can leave him with less room to turn and a harder pass out. The post is a useful change against the right matchup. It occupies a small part of his offense.

## 5. Ball Handling and Playmaking

Wade creates most of his separation with a change of pace, a crossover or the angle of a screen. He can bring the ball up against ordinary pressure and initiate a half-court possession. When he speeds up, the dribble sometimes rises and gives the trailing defender a chance to reach it. He is more secure attacking an opening than standing against a set defender and trying several moves.

He sees the roller and the nearest open shooter. When the help commits early, he can pass across the floor. The difficult read comes a moment sooner, before the help has shown where it will go. He occasionally decides to finish, gets stopped and tries to find a pass while in the air. Those possessions account for part of his 1.2 turnovers per game as a junior.

Against a big staying back, he turns the corner if the lane is open and uses the pull-up if the big protects the rim. Against a hard show, his better possessions keep the dribble alive until the screener can receive the release pass. He will split two defenders when they leave a gap. He also tries it when they have already closed it.

A switch lets him attack a slower big or take a smaller guard toward the post. If the first move produces nothing, he needs to move the ball and start again. Against a trap, he is late recognizing when the sideline has removed his escape route. He can make the pass once he sees it; the work is getting him to see it before both defenders arrive.

He cuts when his defender turns toward the ball and is willing to give it up and get it back. He can screen for another guard, although he sometimes slips away before making the defender change course. Long stretches without a touch can bring an early request for the ball that interrupts the next action.

## 6. Defense

### On the Ball

At the college level, Wade has the feet and strength to stay with most shooting guards. His length lets him contest after giving up a small amount of space. The quickest small guards can make him turn his hips early, then change direction across him. Those matchups require more help than his size might suggest.

Ball screens expose a separate problem. He sometimes meets the screener square and has to chase the play from behind. Getting closer to the screener's hip and following the coverage call would spare him some of those recoveries. His ability to block a shot from behind occasionally saves a possession that began with poor positioning.

### Away from the Ball

Wade can dig at a drive and recover to his man. From the weak side, he reaches shots that a guard would usually leave to a big. His timing also produces deflections and passes he can take the other way. These are useful possessions for a team that wants to run.

He gets into trouble stepping toward the ball before the next rotation is covered. A shooter one pass away can be left open; a cutter can go behind him while he watches the drive. The correction has to name his responsibility on that coverage. Telling him to make fewer gambles leaves too much open to interpretation.

His first NBA assignments should come mainly against guards. He has defended larger players in college, but an NBA forward who can hold position on the block or rebound over him presents a different problem. A coach can try those matchups selectively and judge the possessions.

## 7. Rebounding and Transition

Wade pursues rebounds outside his immediate area and can jump again after the first contest. His 7.1 rebounds per game as a junior include possessions where he collects the ball and starts the break himself. He looks up the floor early. If a teammate has a clear lead, he passes ahead and continues into the play.

With the ball, he runs at the retreating defender until that defender commits. He can finish through a guard or pass to the other lane. His mistakes come when he treats an even-numbered break as if the defense has already lost. An early charge or a pass through the first defender can follow.

The rebounding correction is finding a body before chasing the ball. A larger opponent can move him under the rim if Wade watches the shot. On the offensive glass, his responsibility depends on the lineup. When he is assigned to get back, an unlikely tip attempt can leave the team without a guard behind the ball.

## 8. Practice and Preparation

Wade prefers work that leads back to a possession he recognizes. A drill on keeping the dribble alive has a clear use for him after a game in which he picked it up against a trap. He will repeat the correction. The staff has to check whether he can use it when the defense changes, rather than only against the action he has just practiced.

A shooting period starts with ten catches at each of five perimeter spots. The passer alternates chest passes and low deliveries. Wade repeats the five spots with one-dribble pull-ups, takes twenty free throws and records makes and attempts. In the next period, three offensive players face three defenders. A screener sets the angle. The defenders alternate between staying back, showing and trapping. An assistant records the pass, shot and live-ball turnover on each possession.

Film work returns to the point before the mistake. Wade identifies the screener, the nearest help and the outlet. The coach runs the clip, stops it at the first closing passing lane and asks for the next decision. The same situation goes back onto the court with the coverage changed between repetitions.

After a poor shooting session, he wants more attempts. He may keep shooting when the useful work for that day is finished. His coach sometimes ends the period and puts the decision-making work first at the next practice.

## 9. Communication and Competition

Wade gives short answers to a new interviewer. A question about a particular possession usually gets more from him than a question about leadership. With teammates, he is more willing to joke, argue about a call and explain what he expected on a play.

He responds to a coach who can locate the mistake. On a late pass, he wants to know whether he should have moved it before the screen, during the show or after the screener released. He is less useful in a conversation that stays at the level of effort or focus. He may agree with the criticism without changing the next repetition.

Losing a possession bothers him enough to affect the following one. After a turnover, he can reach for a steal from a poor angle. After a missed shot, he may attack before the offense has its spacing. He usually accepts the correction when the two possessions are shown together. He can make the same mistake later in the game.

He tells the staff he does not read mock drafts. He checks them after each workout. In interviews with teams, he says he expects to earn his minutes. Questions about spending a long stretch off the ball get less definite answers than questions about the pick-and-roll.

## 10. Teammates and Family

At UConn, Wade can let Brown organize the possession and work from the wing. He understands the value of Gordon occupying a defender away from the ball. With Okafor screening, he has to deliver the pass while the rolling lane is available. A late pass makes the big catch in traffic and brings the possession back to Wade's timing.

He asks teammates where they want the ball. He will also question why a screener left early or why a shooter moved out of a passing lane. Those conversations can be useful and can carry into the next practice. He has to hear the same questions about his own decisions.

JoLinda Wade, Dwyane Wade Sr. and his older sister Tragil are part of the family background carried into this version. His father has been involved in his basketball development. Tragil remains someone he consults away from the team. He wants his professional income to make life more secure for the family and wants major decisions explained to them before they appear in the newspaper.

Chicago appeals to him because it is home. His representatives are preparing for other destinations. The family has discussed how many relatives would move if he is selected elsewhere.

## 11. Health and Workload

Wade has appeared in all 98 UConn games. The simulation's medical record contains no surgery, chronic condition or current restriction, and his predraft examinations allow full participation. This medical history belongs to the fictional player.

He is physically developed for 19. His preparation has more immediate use for balance, conditioning and repeated efforts than for adding weight to a 220-pound frame. He needs to keep his dribble and defensive positioning together late in a session, when another burst of speed no longer gets him out of trouble.

His attacks take him into contact frequently. A first-year staff will have to account for that work alongside practices, travel and defensive assignments. His college attendance record gives the team a history to review; the NBA schedule remains untested.

## 12. NBA Role and Development

Wade can enter a rotation at shooting guard, with a chance to start and an experienced organizer beside him. Transition possessions, wing catches and ball screens give him useful work immediately. A coach can give him a smaller group of possessions as the lead handler and add more when he gets through pressure without stopping the offense.

Three parts of his development affect that expansion. He needs the release pass before the trap closes, a dependable catch-and-shoot jumper from NBA distance and better positioning around screens. Each can be examined in ordinary possessions. A difficult make does not answer the first question, and a recovery block does not answer the third.

If his reads against aggressive pressure become faster, he has the scoring and physical ability to become a team's leading perimeter option while handling a substantial share of the offense. If those reads remain late, he may still benefit from another guard beside him in the most difficult possessions. If his shooting also fails to carry over, a team could use him as an attacking reserve.

His coach will need to decide which guards he defends, when he handles against pressure and which pull-up threes are acceptable. Those assignments can change as he develops. They have not been settled at the opening of the simulation.

## 14. Draft Profile and Process

### Declaration and Preparation

Wade declares on May 1 and retains an agent on May 19. He finishes his spring academic work before the main workout schedule and bases his preparation in Chicago. His daily work includes NBA-distance shooting, handling against contact and decisions out of the pick-and-roll.

The historical 2003 early-entry deadline was May 12; the withdrawal deadline was June 19. The draft is June 26 at The Theatre at Madison Square Garden.[^calendar] By the opening date of this profile, Wade has representation and is proceeding into the draft. The meetings, test results and team evaluations below are fictional events within that calendar.

### Chicago Predraft Camp

The Chicago camp runs June 3-6 at Moody Bible Institute.[^camp] Wade attends the medical, measurement and testing appointments associated with the camp period. He does not play in its scrimmages. A high prospect limiting competitive camp participation fits the period's process.[^participation] Teams have his UConn games and their private workouts to evaluate.

He measures 6 feet 4.75 inches without shoes and 6 feet 6 inches in shoes, at 220 pounds. His wingspan is 6 feet 11.5 inches and his standing reach is 8 feet 7.5 inches. The listed playing height elsewhere in this profile is the measurement in shoes.

His standing vertical jump is 32.5 inches, with a maximum vertical of 37 inches. He runs the three-quarter-court sprint in 3.11 seconds and the lane agility drill in 10.74. At the bench press, he completes 12 repetitions with 185 pounds. These are simulation test results, not Wade's historical combine record.

### Private Workouts

On June 2, Chicago works him through handling against contact, transition decisions and shooting after a change of direction. His acceleration and finishing are the best parts of the session. In live ball-screen work, he twice picks up the dribble before the release pass opens. Chicago asks for another look at his handling and passing.

On June 11, Toronto puts him beside another ball handler and asks him to score from catches, cuts and short possessions. He makes 32 of 50 unguarded NBA-distance spot-up threes. His set catches are cleaner than the attempts that require him to turn into the pass. The session adds evidence that he has the range; fifty unguarded attempts provide little basis for a precise NBA shooting percentage.

On June 16, Miami works on getting over screens, recovering to contest and continuing the possession after the first option is removed. Wade holds up against contact from guards. In the passing period, he makes the release earlier when the same coverage returns. A different rotation takes him longer to recognize.

On June 20, Chicago changes the coverage between ball-screen repetitions. Wade keeps his dribble more often than he did on the first visit. He also throws a cross-court pass from below the free-throw line that a long defender intercepts. The staff has both possessions to compare with the first session.

On June 23, Denver tests his half-court scoring against length. He gets separation more easily after a screen than from a stationary isolation. A larger defender absorbs the first contact and makes him use a second move. Denver's evaluation has to weigh his guard creation against the bigger forwards available near the top of the class.

### Interviews

Wade explains his UConn responsibilities in terms of possessions: when Brown initiated, where Gordon was spaced and what he read after Okafor screened. He can identify the turnovers in which he picked up the ball early. He is less definite when asked how he would respond to losing late-game touches for a sustained period.

He expects to earn minutes and continue his education. He asks how a team plans to use him alongside its other guards. His representatives are working from a high-lottery expectation. No team has made a binding commitment to select him.

### Scouting Assessment

The strongest reasons to select him are his ability to reach the paint, finish through guard contact and find a teammate when the help commits. He can rebound and start a break. His weak-side blocks and deflections give him another way to change possession. Three college seasons provide a substantial record for a 19-year-old.

The shooting improvement gives him a way to stay involved when another guard has the ball. His junior season produces 41.3 percent from three and 94.8 percent at the line. The college arc in 2003 is 19 feet 9 inches. NBA distance is 23'9" above the break and 22' in the corners.[^range] An NBA projection has to allow for that distance and for fewer easy catches against longer defenders.

The main offensive question is how well his regular-season control survives more aggressive pressure. His junior assist-to-turnover ratio is 5.98, but his NCAA Tournament turnover average rises to 4.3. That contrast gives teams a reason to test him against traps and changing coverages before giving him full-time point-guard duties immediately. His scoring and passing production are further along than his experience organizing every possession against NBA pressure.

On defense, a team has to distinguish the shots he blocks from the assignments he completes. He has the length to recover and enough strength for guard matchups. He still loses cutters, leaves a nearby shooter to help and gets caught on screens. More consistent positioning would reduce the number of possessions that require him to recover.
